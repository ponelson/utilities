Utilities From the Rate Base Up v1.1
Field guide / v1.1 · 20 October 2026, 5:43 PM ET · build 2026-10-20T17:43-04:00

A utility earns money by spending it.

A regulated electric utility is paid a return on the capital it invests, set by a state commission, and recovers that return through customers' bills. That one arrangement explains why utilities plan trillion-dollar capital programs, why rate cases are the industry's real product launches, why a capacity auction in Pennsylvania shows up on a bill in New Jersey, and why affordability has become the constraint that every other decision now runs into.

01  /  Orientation

What a utility actually is

A monopoly that sells permission to use its wires, priced by the state as a return on the money it has spent.

Most businesses earn money by selling a product for more than it costs. A regulated electric utility earns money in a stranger way. It gets an exclusive franchise to serve a territory and an obligation to serve everyone in it, and in exchange it accepts that a state commission will set its prices. The commission does this by adding up the utility's reasonable costs and then allowing a return on the capital the utility has invested in poles, wires, substations and, in many states, power plants. That invested capital, net of depreciation, is the rate base. The allowed return on it is the utility's profit.

Two consequences follow, and the rest of this guide is mostly about them. First, the utility's earnings grow when its rate base grows, so the business is organized around investing capital and persuading regulators the investment was prudent. Selling more electricity helps at the margin, but spending is what compounds. Second, every dollar of that capital eventually reaches a customer's bill, spread over decades through depreciation and return. When investment accelerates, as it has since 2023, bills rise and regulators, legislators and voters start asking whether all of it was necessary.

Where the opening line breaks

"Earns money by spending it" is a heuristic for investor-owned utilities under cost-of-service regulation, not a law. Spending earns a return only if regulators find it prudent and the plant used and useful; disallowed costs fall on shareholders, and regulatory lag keeps earned returns below allowed ones. Cooperatives and public power earn no equity return at all. Performance-based regulation, where adopted, deliberately weakens the link between capital and profit.

The arrangement is old. The US Supreme Court held in 1877 (Munn v. Illinois) that businesses "affected with a public interest" could be regulated, and states built public utility commissions after 1907 because electric networks behave like natural monopolies: duplicating the wires would cost more than it saves. Energy Architecture in this series covers that history and the physics of the grid. This guide is about the business that grew up inside it.

The spine of the business

Capital spent → plant placed in service → rate base → allowed return × capital structure → revenue requirement → cost allocation to customer classes → rate design → tariffs → the bill → cash collected → interest, dividends, depreciation → capital spent again.

Two links carry most of the argument. Allowed return is set in a contested proceeding, not by a market, and is the single largest lever on earnings. Cost allocation decides which customers pay for what, and it is where households, factories and data centers now fight over who funds the grid being built for load growth.

02  /  Who owns the wires

Four kinds of owner under one set of rules

Shareholders, members, cities and the federal government all own American utilities, and each answers to someone different.

Investor-owned utilities serve most American electricity customers, roughly seven in ten by EIA's count, and they are the companies this guide spends most of its time on because they are the ones whose profits depend on the rate base. But the other three ownership models cover most of the country's land area and shape how costs spread across regions.

Shareholders

Investor-owned utilities

Operating companies regulated by state commissions, usually owned by publicly traded holding companies that may own several utilities across states. Examples: Duke Energy, Southern Company, Xcel, Exelon, American Electric Power. Profit comes from the allowed return on rate base. Most of the debate about bills and returns is about these companies.

Members

Electric cooperatives

About 900 member-owned co-ops, mostly rural, covering more than half the country's land area according to NRECA. Distribution co-ops serve homes and farms and buy power from generation and transmission co-ops they jointly own. Boards elected by members set rates; many states do not regulate co-op rates at all. Margins return to members as capital credits rather than dividends.

Cities

Public power

More than 2,000 community-owned utilities, from large ones like the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, Salt River Project and CPS Energy in San Antonio to small-town departments, serving roughly one in seven US customers (APPA). Governed by city councils or utility boards, financed with tax-exempt bonds, and often transferring money to the city's general budget.

Federal

TVA and the power marketing administrations

The Tennessee Valley Authority is a federal corporation and the largest public power provider. The Bonneville, Western, Southwestern and Southeastern power marketing administrations sell output from federal dams at cost, mostly to co-ops and public power. Their pricing anchors costs across much of the Pacific Northwest and the Southeast.

Why ownership matters for a bill

A co-op or city utility has no shareholders to pay, so it does not earn an equity return, but it still pays interest and must fund capital. It usually borrows more cheaply. Comparisons of bills across ownership types also reflect density: rural co-ops serve far fewer customers per mile of line than urban investor-owned utilities.

03  /  The revenue requirement

The bill starts as a formula

Operating costs plus depreciation plus taxes plus a return on rate base. Every rate case is an argument about one of the terms.

The core of cost-of-service regulation is a single equation for the revenue requirement, the total the utility is allowed to collect in a year:

Revenue requirement = Operating and maintenance expense
                    + Depreciation expense
                    + Taxes (income and other)
                    + Rate base × Weighted average cost of capital

Rate base is roughly the original cost of plant in service, minus accumulated depreciation, minus accumulated deferred income taxes (money the utility has collected for taxes it has not yet paid), plus working capital and certain regulatory assets. Plant generally enters rate base only when it is "used and useful", and the costs must have been prudently incurred. Some states let utilities earn a return on construction work in progress before a plant runs, which shifts risk to customers; Georgia did this for the Vogtle nuclear expansion, completed in 2023 and 2024 at a total project cost reported above $30 billion.

The weighted average cost of capital blends the cost of debt with the allowed return on equity (ROE), weighted by the capital structure the commission approves, typically around half equity. The legal standard comes from two Supreme Court cases, Bluefield (1923) and Hope Natural Gas (1944): the return should be comparable to returns on enterprises of similar risk and sufficient to maintain credit and attract capital. In practice it is set by expert witnesses running discounted cash flow and risk-premium models, consumer advocates arguing for less, and commissions landing near the national average. Because equity returns are taxed and debt interest is not, each dollar of equity return needs roughly a third more in revenue to cover income tax.

A billion dollars of new plant is roughly $125 million of annual revenue requirement. That is why the size of a utility's capital plan is the best single predictor of where its rates are going.

04  /  The proceeding

A rate case is a product launch run as a lawsuit

The utility files, everyone else argues, most cases settle, and the bill changes months later.

A utility starts a rate case by filing its proposed revenue requirement, the evidence behind each component, and new tariffs. It picks a test year: a historical year adjusted for known changes, or a forecast future year, which most utilities prefer because it captures growing costs. Then the intervenors arrive: the commission's own staff, the state consumer advocate, industrial customer groups, environmental organizations, large tech companies, cities and sometimes competitors. Each files testimony attacking parts of the request. Discovery, hearings and an administrative law judge's proposed decision follow; statutes in many states require a final order within about nine to eleven months.

Most cases end in a settlement that grants a share of the request. PowerLines found regulators approved about 58% of the dollars utilities requested over the past two years and rejected very few requests outright: two of 83 in its 2025 sample, with nearly half still pending at the start of 2026. Headlines report the request; the bill reflects the order.

Between rate cases: riders, trackers and deferrals

A growing share of bill changes never goes through a full rate case. Riders are separate line items, adjusted by formula, that pass specific costs straight through: fuel and purchased power (the fuel adjustment clause, which carries no profit), transmission charges, storm recovery, energy efficiency programs, and in many states capital trackers that let the utility start earning on certain investments as soon as they are built. Regulators also allow deferrals, booking a cost as a regulatory asset to be recovered later, often with interest. JCP&L's July 2026 filing in New Jersey asked to recover $476 million of previously deferred storm costs over ten years alongside a $253 million base rate increase.

Mechanism

Regulatory lag

The gap between spending money and recovering it in rates. Lag pushes earned returns below allowed ones and is why utilities press for forecast test years, trackers and multi-year plans.

Mechanism

Decoupling

Separates revenue from sales volume, truing up the difference later, so a utility does not lose money when customers use less. Common for gas utilities and in states promoting efficiency.

Reform

Performance-based regulation

Multi-year rate plans with revenue set by formula and rewards or penalties tied to outcomes such as reliability, interconnection speed or customer service. Hawaii adopted a full framework in 2021; affordability concerns revived interest in 2026.

05  /  The incentive

Why a utility grows by spending

If profit is a return on capital, more capital is more profit. Economists named the bias in 1962.

Harvey Averch and Leland Johnson showed in 1962 that a firm allowed a return on its capital base, when that return exceeds its true cost of capital, will prefer capital-intensive solutions to operating ones. The utility version is familiar to any regulator: a new substation earns a return for forty years; a cheaper demand-response contract or a software upgrade that defers it earns nothing. Commissions counter the bias with prudence reviews, integrated resource plans and, increasingly, rules that let utilities earn on some operating spending. It never disappears.

For investors, the bias is the growth story. Utility holding companies guide to rate base growth, typically mid to high single digits a year, and to earnings per share growth slightly below it. S&P Global data compiled by Gabelli showed median electric utility EPS up 7.0% and dividends up 5.4% in 2025, and electric utilities planning close to $1 trillion of investment over the following three years. The catch is funding: roughly half of new rate base must be financed with equity, and when retained earnings fall short, the company issues shares, diluting the growth.

06  /  Who decides what

Two regulators for one kilowatt-hour

The federal government regulates wholesale power and interstate transmission. States regulate retail rates and distribution. The seam between them is where most fights happen.

The Federal Power Act of 1935 split authority. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) has jurisdiction over sales of electricity for resale and transmission in interstate commerce, which includes the regional markets and almost all high-voltage transmission rates. State commissions regulate what the retail customer pays, the distribution system, and in many states which power plants a utility may build. Energy Architecture explains how this split was born; what matters here is that a single bill contains costs approved in both places, and that large new loads like data centers sit squarely on the boundary.

07  /  Two models of the industry

Vertically integrated or wires-only

Half the country lets one utility own the power plants and the wires. The other half split them apart in the late 1990s.

In a vertically integrated state, one regulated utility owns generation, transmission and distribution, and customers pay for all three through rates set by the state commission. The utility proposes which plants to build in an integrated resource plan, gets a certificate of need, and earns a return on the plants once built. Most of the Southeast, much of the West and parts of the Midwest work this way, even where the utility also participates in a regional market.

In a restructured state, the incumbent utility was required to sell or separate its power plants and became a wires-only company earning a return only on transmission and distribution. Generation is owned by independent power producers selling into competitive markets. Customers can choose a retail supplier; those who do not receive default service, which the utility procures through auctions and passes through at cost. Texas, Pennsylvania, Ohio, New Jersey, Maryland, Illinois, New York and most of New England followed this model after the federal open-access rules of 1996 (FERC Order 888).

08  /  Markets behind the meter

Energy, capacity and the price of being ready

Regional markets pay generators twice: once for the electricity they produce, and in most regions again for promising to be available at the peak.

Where regional transmission organizations run markets, generators are dispatched by price every five minutes. The price at each point on the grid, the locational marginal price, is the cost of serving one more megawatt-hour there: an energy component, plus congestion when the transmission system cannot move cheaper power in, plus losses. Day-ahead markets set most prices; real-time markets settle the differences.

Energy prices alone may not pay for plants that run only a few hours a year, so most eastern markets add a capacity market: an auction, held in advance, that pays resources to be available. PJM, serving 13 states and Washington, DC, runs the largest. Its clearing price went from $28.92 per megawatt-day for the 2024/25 delivery year to $269.92 for 2025/26 as forecast data center demand rose and older plants retired. After Pennsylvania's governor challenged the market, PJM agreed to a price collar. The next three auctions all cleared at the cap: $329.17 for 2026/27, $333.44 for 2027/28 and $325 for 2028/29, announced on 14 July 2026. PJM said the last auction would have cleared at $555 without the cap and still came up 6,831 MW short of its reliability requirement.

Capacity market

PJM, ISO-NE, NYISO, MISO

Forward or seasonal auctions for availability. PJM's is three years forward by design, though the schedule has been compressed; MISO runs a seasonal auction with a sloped demand curve.

Bilateral and planning

CAISO and the non-RTO West and Southeast

09  /  Transmission and distribution

The wires are where the capital goes now

Generation once dominated utility spending. Today most investor-owned capital goes into transmission and distribution, which is also where returns are most secure.

Transmission rates are set by FERC, usually through formula rates that update automatically each year from the utility's actual costs, with little regulatory lag. FERC-set transmission ROEs have generally sat near 10%, plus incentive adders such as 50 basis points for joining a regional transmission organization. That combination of low lag and solid returns makes transmission the most prized asset in the sector. Regional planning is meant to choose the cheapest set of projects; in practice, a large share of spending in some regions goes to "supplemental" or "asset condition" projects that transmission owners plan themselves, which consumer advocates have pushed FERC to scrutinize. FERC's Order 1920 (2024) requires twenty-year regional planning with agreed cost allocation, and implementation runs through the late 2020s.

Distribution is state-regulated and less glamorous: poles, feeders, transformers, meters and substations. Its capital needs have grown with storm hardening, undergrounding in fire and hurricane zones, smart meters, electric vehicle and heat pump load, and connecting rooftop solar and batteries. Distribution investment is where the Averch-Johnson question is sharpest, because the alternative to a new feeder is often a cheaper operating program that earns nothing. Equipment is also scarce: lead times for large power transformers have stretched to years, and grid equipment makers reported surging US orders through 2026.

10  /  Affordability

Why bills are rising, and who argues what about it

Five cost drivers are pushing in the same direction at once, and affordability has become an election issue.

The national average residential electricity price rose about 7.3% between April 2025 and April 2026, according to the North Carolina Clean Energy Technology Center, and PowerLines estimates residential costs are up almost 40% since 2021. The National Energy Assistance Directors Association reports that roughly one in six US households is behind on utility bills, and projected that households heating with electricity would pay about 9% more this coming winter than last.

1

Wires capital

Transmission and distribution investment for reliability, storms, replacement of aging equipment and load growth, recovered through base rates and trackers for decades.

2

Fuel

Natural gas sets the marginal price in most regions. Rising LNG exports and the 2026 disruption of Gulf energy flows, covered in Oil From the Reservoir Up, pushed fuel costs and fuel riders higher. Dominion's 2026 requests included $1.1 billion of unrecovered fuel costs.

3

Capacity

In PJM, the capacity price jump flows directly into default service bills in restructured states.

4

Storms and deferrals

Costs deferred during past storms or rate freezes come due, often securitized or spread over ten years or more.

5

Wildfire mitigation

Undergrounding, vegetation management and insurance in fire-prone states, especially California, where rates are among the highest in the country.

?

Data centers: cause or cushion?

Contested. If large loads pay the full cost of what is built for them, their fixed-cost contributions can lower rates for others; if costs are socialized or forecasts prove inflated, households pay for capacity they did not need.

The positions

Utilities and their trade group, the Edison Electric Institute, argue that investment is required for reliability, severe weather and demand growth, that every request receives rigorous review by state regulators, and that large-load tariffs should ensure data centers pay their share. Consumer advocates and groups such as PowerLines argue that regulators approve most of what is requested, that allowed returns are higher than investors require, and that the capital bias inflates spending. Data center developers argue they are paying for the grid they use and bringing tax revenue and fixed-cost contributions. State responses in 2026 included rate freezes, expanded bill assistance, performance-based ratemaking proposals and new tariffs for large users. With federal midterm elections on 3 November 2026, energy affordability became a campaign issue across parties.

11  /  Data centers from the utility side

The biggest customers anyone has seen, and the hardest to forecast

A single campus can equal a mid-sized city's demand. The utility must decide how much to build before knowing how much will show up.

For a utility, a data center request is a forecasting and risk-allocation problem. Developers file requests with several utilities for the same project, so pipelines overstate real demand. If the utility builds generation and transmission for load that never arrives, the cost lands on remaining customers unless the contract says otherwise. That is why large-load tariffs spread rapidly in 2025 and 2026: minimum demand charges for ten to twenty years, exit fees, collateral, and longer notice periods. Dirt to Tokens catalogs those tariffs state by state; the utility-side logic is that the contract has to make the customer, not the household, the guarantor of the investment.

Federal regulators moved into the space quickly. In October 2025 the Energy Secretary directed FERC to consider rules for connecting loads above 20 MW to the transmission system. FERC issued an order on co-located generation and load in PJM in December 2025, and on 18 June 2026 issued show cause orders requiring each FERC-regulated grid operator to justify or revise its large-load and co-location rules. In PJM, the 6,831 MW shortfall prompted a one-time reliability backstop procurement. On 29 September 2026 FERC accepted its framework but suspended it for five months over cost allocation, transmission-owner exit rules and collateral, saying the plan had to do more to make large loads pay; Chair Laura Swett called it a "deeply flawed, eleventh-hour procurement mechanism." PJM said it would refile by 29 October.

Utilities are also signing direct deals. AEP announced a 960 MW advanced nuclear arrangement in Indiana with Amazon and X-energy in October 2026, and NextEra signed supply deals with Google and Meta in 2025. These deals can shift risk to the tech company, or quietly onto the rate base, depending on how the commission approves them.

12  /  What breaks a utility

Wildfire turned a low-risk business into a high-risk one in the West

Regulated returns assume limited downside. Liability for catastrophic fires removed that assumption for utilities in fire country.

California applies inverse condemnation to utilities: if utility equipment is a substantial cause of a fire, the utility can be liable for property damage regardless of negligence. After the 2017 and 2018 fires, including the Camp Fire that destroyed Paradise, PG&E filed for bankruptcy in January 2019. The state responded in 2019 (AB 1054) with a Wildfire Fund jointly financed by shareholders and customers and a cap on shareholder reimbursement of 20% of a utility's equity rate base, about $4.3 billion for Southern California Edison, if the utility is found imprudent. That cap disappears if the fund runs out.

The January 2025 Eaton Fire in Altadena, which investigators have tied to Edison equipment, tested the arrangement. SB 254 (2025) added a continuation account of up to $18 billion for fires after 19 September 2025 and allowed securitization of 2025 fire claims. By April 2026 Edison had been named in about 2,000 Eaton lawsuits and offered more than $500 million through its compensation program; the first jury trial is set for January 2027. In September 2026 the California legislature adjourned without passing the follow-on liability reform the governor and utilities had sought; Fitch moved Edison's outlook to negative, and PG&E said it would defer $2 billion of work.

13  /  Finance and consolidation

How utilities are financed, bought and sold

Regulated operating companies borrow against their assets; holding companies raise equity; infrastructure funds buy pieces; and scale has become a strategy again.

The standard structure is a publicly traded holding company owning several state-regulated operating companies. Each operating company issues its own secured bonds (often first mortgage bonds) and keeps a regulator-approved capital structure. The holding company issues additional debt and the equity that the operating companies need, through follow-on offerings, at-the-market programs and hybrid securities that rating agencies count partly as equity. Credit ratings turn on metrics such as funds from operations to debt and on the agencies' view of each state's regulation, so a hostile rate order in one state can raise borrowing costs for the whole group.

Three financing patterns have spread with the capital cycle. Securitization moves specific costs (storms, retired plants, fuel spikes) into highly rated bonds backed by a dedicated charge on bills, which lowers the financing cost but locks in the charge. Minority stake sales bring in infrastructure capital without issuing common stock; AEP sold a 19.9% interest in two of its transmission companies to KKR and PSP Investments in early 2025 for about $2.8 billion. Take-privates remove utilities from public markets altogether: ALLETE agreed in 2024 to be acquired by Canada Pension Plan Investment Board and Global Infrastructure Partners, a deal Minnesota regulators approved in 2025, and Blackstone Infrastructure agreed in 2025 to buy TXNM Energy.

Consolidation returned at scale in 2026. On 18 May NextEra agreed to combine with Dominion Energy in an all-stock deal at an exchange ratio of 0.8138 NextEra shares per Dominion share, valuing Dominion's equity at about $66.8 billion and leaving Dominion holders with about 25.5% of the combined company. The companies proposed about $2.25 billion of customer bill credits over two years after closing. The deal needs approval from FERC and from regulators in Virginia, North Carolina and South Carolina, and watchdog groups raised questions in October about NextEra's political donations while federal review was pending. Merger approvals are where commissions extract concessions, and where customers should read the long-term rate commitments rather than the headline credits.

14  /  Tricks of the trade

The promotional playbook

Utility communications rarely misstate facts. They choose which facts to put first.

Growth

Rate base growth without the equity plan

An 8% rate base growth target means little until you see how much new stock funds it. Ask for the equity issuance plan and the payout ratio alongside the capital plan; Model 2 shows how quickly dilution erodes per-share growth.

Returns

Allowed ROE quoted as if it were earned

An authorized return is an opportunity, not a result. Central Maine Power, for example, earned 5.8% in 2025 against a 9.35% authorization, per testimony in its 2026 rate case. The gap between allowed and earned is where regulatory lag and disallowances show up.

Demand

Load forecasts built on requests, not contracts

Large-load pipelines include duplicate and speculative requests. A forecast that counts every inquiry justifies more capital than one counting signed service agreements with minimum bills. FERC told PJM in September 2026 to use more up-to-date forecasts in allocating backstop costs.

Accounting

Regulatory assets that keep growing

Deferred storm, fuel and other costs sit on the balance sheet as assets because the utility expects to recover them later. A rising balance is a bill that has been postponed, not avoided.

Bills

"Typical bill" comparisons

Bill-impact figures can use low monthly usage, exclude riders, or cover only base rates. Ask for the total bill at average residential use, with every rider, before and after.

Settlements

"Less than requested" as the headline

Utilities often ask for more than they expect. A settlement at 60% of the request can still be the largest increase in the utility's history.

Mergers

Bill credits as the price of approval

One-time credits are visible and politically useful. The commitments that matter are rate freezes, limits on recovering acquisition premiums and goodwill, and ring-fencing of the operating company's credit.

Cost allocation

"Large customers will pay" without the tariff

Promises that data centers will cover their costs mean something only when the tariff sets minimum demand charges, contract length, exit fees and collateral. Read the tariff, not the press release.

Constellation (which agreed in 2025 to acquire Calpine), Vistra, NRG, Talen. Not utilities, but the sellers into the markets whose prices utilities and default service customers pay.

Grid operators

ISOs and RTOs

PJM, MISO, ERCOT, CAISO, SPP, NYISO, ISO New England. Nonprofit operators of regional grids and markets; their auction rules and transmission plans move billions between customers and producers.

Referees

Regulators and reliability

FERC (chaired by Laura Swett since late 2025), state commissions and their association NARUC, NERC, and state consumer advocates (NASUCA nationally).

Advocates and analysts

The voices in the room

Edison Electric Institute (investor-owned utilities), APPA (public power), PowerLines and other consumer groups, environmental intervenors, large-customer coalitions, and Regulatory Research Associates, whose rate case data the industry quotes.

16  /  The present moment

Where utilities stand in October 2026

Record capital plans, record rate requests, a capacity market at its cap, and an election three weeks away.

Data as of 20 October 2026. Figures and events in this section were checked against the sources listed at the end of the page on or before that date. Treat this section as a dated snapshot; the explanatory sections are evergreen.

1. Rates

Investor-owned utilities had requested $23.1 billion of electric and gas rate increases in the first three quarters of 2026, including a record $9.2 billion in the second quarter, according to PowerLines. The South accounted for about $9 billion of the year's requests. Authorized returns did not move: RRA put the average electric ROE at 9.84% for the first half, the same as 2025, with individual cases ranging from an 8.20% administrative law judge recommendation for Consumers Energy in Michigan to requests above 10%.

2. PJM

The 2028/29 capacity auction cleared at the $325 cap for the third straight year, short 6,831 MW of its reliability requirement, with PJM estimating an uncapped price of $555 (and $777 in northern Illinois). FERC suspended the one-time backstop procurement on 29 September for five months to fix cost allocation; PJM planned to refile by 29 October. The 2029/30 auction is scheduled for December 2026 under the same collar.

3. Large loads

FERC's 18 June show cause orders put every FERC-regulated grid operator's large-load and co-location rules under review. States kept adding large-load tariffs. Utilities and tech companies kept signing direct supply deals, including AEP's October nuclear arrangement with Amazon and X-energy.

4. Consolidation

The NextEra and Dominion combination announced in May was working through FERC and three state commissions, with proposed bill credits of about $2.25 billion and scrutiny from watchdog groups. Infrastructure funds remained active buyers of minority stakes and whole companies.

5. Wildfire

California's legislature adjourned in September without passing utility liability reform, Fitch moved Edison International to a negative outlook, and PG&E deferred $2 billion of work. The first Eaton Fire jury trial is set for January 2027.

6. Markets and politics

Utilities were the worst-performing S&P 500 sector through September after two strong years, even as their capital plans grew. With midterm elections on 3 November, utility bills, data center costs and returns on equity had become campaign topics, and several states were weighing rate freezes, assistance programs and performance-based regulation.

17  /  Vocabulary

The words, defined

Filter by category or search. Utility vocabulary is mostly legal and accounting language wearing a hard hat.

Regulatory compact Regulation

The informal bargain behind utility regulation: an exclusive territory and a chance to earn a fair return, in exchange for an obligation to serve all customers at regulated prices.

Public utility commission Regulation

The state body, called a PUC, PSC or similar, that sets retail rates and oversees utilities. Commissioners are appointed in most states and elected in about ten.

Franchise territory Regulation

The area in which a utility has the exclusive right, and duty, to provide service.

Obligation to serve Regulation

The duty to connect and supply any customer in the territory on the terms of the tariff.

Rate case Regulation

The formal proceeding in which a commission sets a utility's revenue requirement and rates.

Test year Regulation

The twelve-month period whose costs and sales are used to set rates. Historical test years use actual data; future test years use forecasts.

Intervenor Regulation

Any party admitted to a rate case besides the utility: commission staff, consumer advocates, industrial groups, environmental organizations, cities, large customers.

Settlement Regulation

An agreement among some or all parties resolving a rate case, which the commission may accept, modify or reject. Also called a stipulation.

Prudence review Regulation

A commission's examination of whether costs were reasonable given what the utility knew when it made the decision.

Used and useful Regulation

The principle that plant enters rate base only when it is in service and benefits customers.

Disallowance Regulation

A commission's refusal to let a utility recover a cost from customers, which falls instead on shareholders.

Integrated resource plan Regulation

A utility's long-range plan for meeting demand, filed with the commission in vertically integrated states and many others.

Certificate of public convenience and necessity Regulation

State approval to build a major facility such as a power plant or transmission line.

Performance-based regulation Regulation

Rate frameworks that set revenue by formula over several years and tie rewards or penalties to measured outcomes.

Multi-year rate plan Regulation

Rates set for several years at once, usually with scheduled step increases, reducing the frequency of rate cases.

Formula rate Regulation

A rate that updates annually from actual costs by an approved formula, common for FERC-regulated transmission.

Regulatory lag Regulation

The delay between when a utility incurs costs and when they are reflected in rates.

Decoupling Regulation

A mechanism that separates allowed revenue from sales volume, truing up over- or under-collection later.

Construction work in progress Regulation

Capital spent on projects not yet in service. Some states allow a return on it before completion, shifting risk to customers.

Administrative law judge Regulation

The hearing officer who manages a rate case record and issues a proposed decision for the commission.

Consumer advocate Regulation

A state office or official representing residential and small business customers in utility proceedings.

Federal Power Act Regulation

The 1935 law dividing federal and state authority over electricity; section 203 governs FERC review of utility mergers and asset transfers.

Revenue requirement Rates & bills

The total annual revenue a utility is allowed to collect: operating costs, depreciation, taxes and a return on rate base.

Cost of service study Rates & bills

The analysis that allocates the revenue requirement among customer classes according to how each causes costs.

Customer class Rates & bills

A group of customers with a common tariff, such as residential, small commercial, large industrial or large load.

Rate design Rates & bills

How a class's share of costs is turned into charges: fixed, per kWh, per kW of demand, by time of day.

Fixed customer charge Rates & bills

A monthly charge that does not vary with use, covering meters, billing and part of the distribution system.

Volumetric charge Rates & bills

A charge per kilowatt-hour used.

Demand charge Rates & bills

A charge per kilowatt of a customer's highest usage in a period, common for commercial and industrial customers.

Time-of-use rate Rates & bills

Prices that vary by time of day or season to reflect higher costs at peak.

Rider Rates & bills

A separate charge that recovers a specific cost by formula outside a full rate case.

Fuel adjustment clause Rates & bills

A rider passing fuel and purchased power costs through to customers without profit, trued up periodically.

Capital tracker Rates & bills

A rider that lets a utility recover the costs of, and return on, specific investments as they are placed in service.

Regulatory asset Rates & bills

A cost the commission allows a utility to defer and recover later, carried on the balance sheet as an asset.

Default service Rates & bills

Supply provided to customers who do not choose a retail supplier in restructured states, procured by the utility and passed through at cost.

Net metering Rates & bills

Crediting customers with rooftop solar for power they export, often at the retail rate; a long-running fight over cost shifting.

Cross-subsidy Rates & bills

When one customer class pays more than its cost of service so another can pay less.

Large-load tariff Rates & bills

A rate schedule for very large customers such as data centers, typically with minimum bills, long contracts, exit fees and collateral.

Minimum demand charge Rates & bills

A floor on what a large customer pays regardless of actual use, protecting other customers if load falls short.

Exit fee Rates & bills

A payment owed by a customer that leaves before its contract term, covering unrecovered investment made to serve it.

Bill credit Rates & bills

A one-time or temporary reduction on bills, common as a condition of merger approval.

Rate base Finance

The net investment on which a utility may earn a return: plant in service less accumulated depreciation and deferred taxes, plus working capital and certain regulatory assets.

Accumulated deferred income taxes Finance

Taxes collected from customers but not yet paid, usually because of accelerated tax depreciation; deducted from rate base.

Allowed return on equity Finance

The return on shareholders' share of rate base that a commission authorizes when setting rates.

Earned return on equity Finance

The return the utility actually achieves, after lag, disallowances, weather and cost overruns.

Weighted average cost of capital Finance

The blend of allowed equity return and cost of debt, weighted by the approved capital structure, applied to rate base.

Capital structure Finance

The mix of debt and equity a commission uses to set rates, typically around half equity.

Carrying charge Finance

The annual revenue requirement per dollar of rate base, combining return, depreciation and taxes; often around 12% to 13% at current returns.

Averch-Johnson effect Finance

The tendency of a firm earning a regulated return on capital to over-invest in capital relative to operating alternatives.

Holding company Finance

The parent, usually publicly traded, that owns one or more regulated operating companies and raises equity for them.

Operating company Finance

The regulated utility that owns the assets, serves customers and issues its own secured debt.

First mortgage bonds Finance

Debt secured by a lien on the operating company's utility plant, the cheapest debt in the structure.

Hybrid securities Finance

Long-dated subordinated debt that rating agencies treat as partly equity, used to fund growth without issuing common stock.

At-the-market program Finance

A facility that lets a company sell new shares gradually into the market at prevailing prices.

Funds from operations to debt Finance

A cash flow coverage ratio central to utility credit ratings; falling below an agency's threshold invites a downgrade.

Securitization Finance

Issuing highly rated bonds backed by a dedicated, nonbypassable charge on bills to recover specific costs such as storms or retired plants.

Take-private Finance

The purchase of a listed utility by private investors, typically infrastructure and pension funds.

Minority stake sale Finance

Selling a non-controlling share of a utility subsidiary to raise equity without issuing common stock.

Payout ratio Finance

The share of earnings paid as dividends; the remainder funds part of the equity needed for rate base growth.

ISO and RTO Markets

Independent system operator and regional transmission organization: nonprofits that run regional grids and wholesale markets.

Locational marginal price Markets

The wholesale price at a specific grid point: the cost of serving one more MWh there, including congestion and losses.

Congestion Markets

When transmission limits prevent cheaper power from reaching a location, raising its local price.

Day-ahead market Markets

The market that schedules and prices most electricity the day before delivery; real-time markets settle the differences.

Capacity market Markets

An auction paying resources to be available to meet future peak demand.

Base Residual Auction Markets

PJM's main capacity auction for a future delivery year.

Price collar Markets

A cap and floor on auction clearing prices, adopted in PJM after the 2025/26 price jump.

Resource adequacy Markets

Having enough available supply to meet demand plus a reserve under a reliability standard, often one outage day in ten years.

Reserve margin Markets

Available capacity above forecast peak demand, as a percentage of the peak.

Energy-only market Markets

A market without a capacity auction, relying on high prices during scarcity to pay for availability; ERCOT is the main US example.

Scarcity pricing Markets

Rules that raise prices toward a cap when reserves run short, signalling the value of available supply.

Independent power producer Markets

A non-utility owner of generation selling into markets or under contract.

Power purchase agreement Markets

A long-term contract to buy output from a specific generator at agreed prices.

Co-location Markets

Placing a large load at or beside a power plant, sharing its connection; FERC set new PJM rules for it in December 2025.

Backstop procurement Markets

A one-time purchase of new capacity outside the regular auction to close a reliability shortfall, as PJM proposed in 2026.

Transmission Grid

High-voltage lines and substations moving bulk power over distance, priced mainly under FERC jurisdiction.

Distribution Grid

Lower-voltage lines, transformers and meters delivering power to customers, regulated by states.

Peak load contribution Grid

A customer's share of the system's annual peak demand, used to allocate capacity and some transmission costs.

Interconnection queue Grid

The ordered list of projects waiting for studies and approval to connect to the grid.

Supplemental project Grid

A transmission project planned by a transmission owner outside the regional process, often for local reliability or asset replacement.

Grid hardening Grid

Investment to make lines and equipment withstand storms, fire and heat, from stronger poles to undergrounding.

Investor-owned utility Ownership

A shareholder-owned utility regulated by a state commission; serves most US customers.

Electric cooperative Ownership

A utility owned by its members, governed by an elected board, usually rural.

Generation and transmission cooperative Ownership

A co-op owned by distribution co-ops that supplies their power and runs their transmission.

Public power Ownership

Utilities owned by cities, counties, states or districts, financed by tax-exempt bonds.

Power marketing administration Ownership

A federal agency selling power from federal dams at cost, such as Bonneville and Western.

Wires-only utility Ownership

A utility that owns transmission and distribution but not generation, typical of restructured states.

Vertically integrated utility Ownership

A utility owning generation, transmission and distribution, with all three in regulated rates.

Restructuring Ownership

The late-1990s separation of generation from wires in many states, with retail choice and competitive wholesale markets.

Inverse condemnation Risk

A California doctrine making utilities liable for property damage from fires their equipment causes, regardless of negligence.

Wildfire Fund Risk

California's shareholder and customer funded pool, created in 2019 and extended in 2025, that pays eligible claims from utility-caused fires.

Public safety power shutoff Risk

Deliberately de-energizing lines during extreme fire weather to prevent ignitions.

Stranded cost Risk

Investment that can no longer be recovered as planned, because a plant closed early, a customer left, or a market changed.

18  /  What to retain

Twelve things worth keeping

If everything else evaporates, these survive.

1

Utilities sell capital

The product is electricity; the profit is a regulated return on invested capital.

2

The bill is a formula

Operating costs, depreciation, taxes and a return on rate base. Every fight is about one term.

3

Remember the carrying charge

Each dollar of rate base costs customers about 12 to 13 cents a year at current returns.

4

Allowed is not earned

Lag, disallowances and weather push earned returns below authorized ones.

5

Riders move bills quietly

A growing share of increases arrives through trackers and deferrals, not rate cases.

6

The capital bias is structural

Averch and Johnson described it in 1962; regulators manage it, never remove it.

7

Equity funding sets EPS

Rate base growth becomes per-share growth only to the extent new equity is avoided.

8

Two regulators, one bill

FERC sets wholesale and transmission; states set retail and distribution.

9

Market structure decides exposure

Restructured states feel capacity prices directly; integrated states feel them through plants and riders.

10

Cost allocation is the fight

Who pays for what is built for large loads is the decade's central regulatory question.

11

Liability can break the model

Wildfire showed that a regulated return cannot absorb unlimited downside.

12

Affordability now binds

Record capital plans meet record requests and an electorate paying attention.
